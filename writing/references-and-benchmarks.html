Finding references and a bit about benchmarking

5 min read

Everyone collects references, but too often it ends with a folder of nice-looking screens that does nothing to solve the task. In this guide I’ll show a different way to search: one where every find answers a specific question.

A reference is a single example of someone else’s solution: a screen, a flow or a piece of copy. Benchmarking is the process of comparing how different products solve the same task and working out why a solution works.

What matters is how a solution works, not only how it looks. Copy the visuals and you get a pretty screen. Understand the logic and you get an idea you can adapt to your own product.

Three things worth knowing before you start

Goal. The outcome the user and the business need.

Context. Product type, B2B or B2C, audience experience, devices.

Constraints. Technical, legal and resource limits, plus the client’s expectations. They keep you from searching for the impossible, and later they filter what you’ve collected.

Identify the service type and the funnel stage

The service type and the funnel stage define the goal of a screen, and so they define which products are comparable. SaaS onboarding and a store checkout solve different tasks, even if both look like a multi-step form.

Once you know the stage, you stop looking for “fintech references” and start looking for “onboarding and activation in SaaS”. The pool of suitable products grows beyond your industry, and the search itself gets more precise: you’re comparing solutions to the same task.

Describe the scenario and decide whose products to look at

Compare scenarios, not whole products. First describe yours without any interface: what the user wants to get and which steps it takes.

Then look for products that already solve it, moving from near to far:

Direct competitors. The same product for the same audience.

Adjacent products. The same category, another price segment or audience.

Neighboring fields. The same mechanic elsewhere: document checks exist in banks, car sharing and home rentals.

Market leaders. From any industry. Their solutions are usually backed by research.

Break the task into familiar patterns

Most tasks are assembled from parts that have been solved many times: a catalog, a detail page, a cart. People know how these parts work from other products and expect yours to work the same way (Jakob’s Law). So name the patterns in your task and look for references to each one. A catalog is needed where people don’t know exactly what they’re looking for: they narrow the selection step by step until they reach the right item. If they know the name, search is enough.

Example. We’re designing a trip planning app. A tourist doesn’t know what the city offers, so the list of places is a catalog. A place page with photos and opening hours is a detail page. The places added to the trip work like a cart. Now references can come from online stores and booking services, not only from other travel apps.

Exception. In niches with complex business logic (geo services, social networks, messengers) abstraction helps less. There you look for proven solutions from direct analogs: the systems are too complex to invent the connections from scratch.

Behind this is Jakob’s Law: people spend most of their time in other products and expect yours to work the same way. Structure, behavior and controls should feel familiar; your own visual style doesn’t get in the way of that.

Collect everything that catches your eye

Save everything you like and don’t judge it yet. Filter too early and you miss an unexpected find from another niche.

Combine sources. Walk through the flows yourself to see errors, emails and what happens on return. Use screen libraries like Mobbin to compare one pattern across dozens of products. Check usability research from Baymard Institute and Nielsen Norman Group.

Label each screen right away: product, step, pattern.

Analyze what you collected

A reference without a note is just a picture. For each one, write down what fits your task, what doesn’t and why.

Ask three questions: which user and business task does it solve, what happens before and after it, and is it made for an audience like yours?

Compare flows by the same criteria in a product × criterion table. What everyone shares is the market norm. Where solutions differ is room for your own hypotheses.

Cut what you don’t need

Run what you’ve collected through two filters.

Constraints. Remove what doesn’t fit the technical, legal and resource limits, however well it works for others.

Value and cost. Weigh how much a solution helps the user against what it costs to build. A large feature with no clear value is the first to go.

Check your conclusions with the team

One person’s selection stays a hypothesis until others have seen it. Show the analysis to the product manager, the developers and the client: they know priorities and constraints you may not.

Benchmarks don’t prove a solution will work for you. The final check is user testing and analytics after launch.

What you end up with

A list of the patterns the task is made of, a product × criterion table, annotated references (what you take, what you rejected and why) and a list of questions nobody asked when the task was first discussed.

Doing it faster with Mobbin and Claude

Mobbin has an MCP server, so Claude can search its library of real screens and flows for me. I describe the task, send a screenshot or attach a brief, and get examples from other products with links back to Mobbin.

To keep every search in the order of this guide, I packed the steps into a Claude skill. It turns a task into search queries, checks each result against one criterion and says what it did not find.

It speeds up collecting, not thinking. Notes, comparison and cuts are still my job.
